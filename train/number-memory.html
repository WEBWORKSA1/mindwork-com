Number memory test

Remember a number that grows by one digit each round.

What it measures

Digit span: how many digits you can hold in short-term memory long enough to repeat them. It leans on the verbal part of working memory, the inner voice that rehearses a phone number while you look for a pen.

The science behind it

George Miller’s 1956 paper put the typical span at “seven, plus or minus two” items. Later work by Nelson Cowan argued the true limit is closer to four chunks once rehearsal and grouping are prevented. Both can be right: a chunk can hold several digits, so 1-9-4-5 is four items to one person and a single year to another.

Chunking is also why span is trainable on this exact task. In a well-known study by Ericsson, Chase and Faloon, a student raised his digit span from about 7 to about 80 over many months by recoding digits as running times. His memory for letters did not improve at all.

How to improve your score

  • Group digits in threes or fours, the way phone numbers are written.
  • Say the groups in a rhythm under your breath. Rehearsal keeps the sound trace alive.
  • Attach meaning where you can: years, prices, ages, sports scores.
  • Look away from the screen the moment the number disappears and repeat it once before typing.

Reading your result

ResultWhat we call it
12 or more digitsExceptional
9 to 11 digitsStrong
6 to 8 digitsTypical
5 or fewer digitsBelow the usual range

These ranges are informal guides for this browser version of the task. They are not clinical norms, and a single run says little; look at your trend.

Sources
  • Miller, G. A. (1956). The magical number seven, plus or minus two. Psychological Review.
  • Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory. Behavioral and Brain Sciences.
  • Ericsson, K. A., Chase, W. G., & Faloon, S. (1980). Acquisition of a memory skill. Science.
