Sequence memory test

Repeat a growing pattern of tiles. One slip ends the run.

What it measures

Visuospatial sequence memory: holding a series of locations, in order, for a few seconds. It uses the spatial side of working memory, the part you rely on to remember a route or the order of steps on a screen.

The science behind it

This is a browser version of the Corsi block-tapping task, introduced by Philip Corsi in 1972 and still used in clinics and labs. An examiner taps a set of blocks in order and the person taps them back; the longest sequence repeated correctly is the span. Healthy adults usually manage about five or six blocks on the original task.

In Baddeley and Hitch’s model of working memory, tasks like this draw on a “visuospatial sketchpad” that is separate from the verbal loop used for digits. That is why your number-memory and sequence-memory results can differ a lot.

How to improve your score

  • Turn the sequence into a path or a shape: an L, a zigzag, a loop.
  • Name positions as you watch (top left, centre, bottom right) to recruit verbal memory as a backup.
  • Follow with your eyes, not your cursor. Moving the mouse during playback splits attention.
  • Keep a steady tapping rhythm. Rushing the last two tiles is the usual way runs end.

Reading your result

ResultWhat we call it
13 or more stepsExceptional
9 to 12 stepsStrong
5 to 8 stepsTypical
4 or fewer stepsEarly exit

These ranges are informal guides for this browser version of the task. They are not clinical norms, and a single run says little; look at your trend.

Sources
  • Corsi, P. M. (1972). Human memory and the medial temporal region of the brain. Doctoral thesis, McGill University.
  • Baddeley, A. D., & Hitch, G. (1974). Working memory. In The Psychology of Learning and Motivation.
