Reaction time test

Click when the pad turns green. Five trials, averaged in milliseconds.

What it measures

Simple visual reaction time: the gap between a signal appearing and your response. The number bundles four things together: how fast your eyes register the change, how fast your brain decides, how fast your finger moves, and the lag in your screen and input device.

The science behind it

Reaction time is one of the oldest measurements in psychology; Francis Galton collected thousands of readings in his London laboratory in the 1880s. In controlled lab studies healthy young adults usually answer a visual signal in roughly 200 to 250 milliseconds. Browser tests run slower, because displays, wireless mice and touchscreens each add delay.

The number moves with your state. Fatigue, alcohol and divided attention slow it, and so does choice: Hick’s law says decision time rises with the logarithm of the number of options. That is why this test uses one signal and one response.

How to improve your score

  • Run one warm-up round before the round you count. First attempts are usually the slowest.
  • Use a wired mouse on a desktop monitor if you can. Hardware lag is often 30 to 80 ms.
  • Test at the same time of day when you compare runs. Alertness changes through the day.
  • Do not guess the timing. Early clicks are discarded, and guessing makes the average meaningless.

Reading your result

ResultWhat we call it
Under 200 msExceptional
200 to 249 msFast
250 to 299 msTypical
300 to 399 msOn the slow side
400 ms or moreSlow run, usually fatigue or distraction

These ranges are informal guides for this browser version of the task. They are not clinical norms, and a single run says little; look at your trend.

Sources
  • Hick, W. E. (1952). On the rate of gain of information. Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology.
  • Woods, D. L., Wyma, J. M., Yund, E. W., Herron, T. J., & Reed, B. (2015). Factors influencing the latency of simple reaction time. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience.
