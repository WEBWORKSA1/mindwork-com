Deep work: a protocol you can run this week

A five-step routine for getting two hours of real concentration into an ordinary working day. About 6 minutes to read.

Cal Newport defines deep work as professional activity performed in a state of distraction-free concentration that pushes your abilities to their limit. The definition matters less than the observation behind it: the work that creates most of your value needs long, unbroken attention, and the ordinary working day is built to prevent it.

You do not need a cabin or a sabbatical. You need a small, repeatable routine. This one takes about ten minutes to set up each day.

Step 1. Decide the session the day before

At the end of each day, write down one task for tomorrow’s deep session and the first physical action it starts with. “Draft section 2 of the proposal: open the outline and write the problem statement” beats “work on proposal”. Deciding in advance removes the choice at the moment you are most tempted to check email instead.

Step 2. Put it on the calendar, early

Block 90 to 120 minutes at the time your energy is highest, which for most people is the first half of the morning. Treat the block as a meeting with someone you would not cancel on. The time-block planner will lay out the rest of the day around it.

Step 3. Remove the exits

Close email and chat completely; do not just minimise them. Put your phone in another room or in a bag. Sophie Leroy’s research on attention residue shows why this matters: after switching away from a task, part of your attention stays behind with it. A glance at a message is a switch, and it costs you several minutes of full capacity.

Step 4. Work in two or three timed sessions

Use the focus timer at 50 minutes with a 10-minute break, or 25 and 5 if 50 feels long at first. In the break, stand up and look at something far away. Do not open a feed; a feed is another task and leaves its own residue.

Step 5. Close the session on paper

When the block ends, write two lines: what you finished and the next action. This gives your mind permission to let go, and it makes tomorrow’s Step 1 trivial.

How much is enough?

Less than you think. In the study of violinists that launched the research on deliberate practice, Anders Ericsson and colleagues found that the best students practised about three and a half hours a day, in sessions of no more than about 90 minutes. Newport suggests that around four hours a day is the ceiling for most knowledge workers, and that beginners should expect one. Two solid hours, five days a week, is ten hours of your best thinking. Most people get close to none.

When it fails

  • You get interrupted. Say when you will be available: “I am in the middle of something; can I come to you at 11?” Most requests survive a 45-minute delay.
  • You cannot start. Shrink the first action until it is trivially small, and run a two-minute attention warm-up first.
  • Your day is all meetings. Protect one block a week and defend it. Then use the track record of what that block produced to ask for a second.
Sources
  • Newport, C. (2016). Deep Work: Rules for Focused Success in a Distracted World.
  • Leroy, S. (2009). Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes.
  • Ericsson, K. A., Krampe, R. T., & Tesch-Römer, C. (1993). The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance. Psychological Review.
