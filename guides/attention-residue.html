Attention residue: why switching tasks costs more than you think

What happens in your head when you jump between tasks, how much it costs, and four ways to cut the bill. About 5 minutes to read.

You are writing a report. A message arrives; you answer it in forty seconds and return to the report. It feels free. It is not. For the next several minutes part of your mind is still on the message, and your work on the report is slower and worse.

The research

Sophie Leroy named this effect attention residue in a 2009 paper. In her experiments, people who moved to a second task before finishing the first performed worse on the second, because thoughts about the unfinished task kept intruding. The effect was strongest when the first task was left incomplete and without a deadline.

Laboratory work on task switching explains the mechanism. Rubinstein, Meyer and Evans showed in 2001 that every switch has two costs: changing goals (“I am doing this now, not that”) and loading the rules for the new task. Each cost is a fraction of a second, but they multiply across a day of switching, and they grow as tasks get more complex.

In real offices the numbers are larger. Gloria Mark’s field studies of information workers found that people switched activities every few minutes, and that after a real interruption it took more than twenty minutes on average to return to the original task. In a 2008 experiment her team found that interrupted people actually worked faster to compensate, and paid for it with more stress, frustration and effort.

Four ways to cut the cost

  1. Finish or park. Before switching, either reach a natural stopping point or write one line: where you are and what comes next. Leroy’s later work found that a short “ready-to-resume” note reduces residue.
  2. Batch the small stuff. Check email and chat at set times, three or four a day, instead of continuously. Each batch is one switch instead of thirty.
  3. Make interruptions expensive. Close the apps, silence notifications, and put the phone out of reach during focus blocks. The focus timer gives the block an edge.
  4. Single-task meetings. A meeting attended while answering messages costs the meeting and the messages. Decline it or attend it.

Measure it yourself

Run the Stroop test after an hour of uninterrupted work, and again after an hour of constant switching. Interference scores usually rise when attention is fragmented. One pair of runs proves little, but a week of them will show you your own pattern.

The exception

Some jobs are interruptions: support, operations, management of a busy team. If that is yours, the aim is not zero switching. Protect one block for the work that needs depth, and accept the rest as the job.

Sources
  • Leroy, S. (2009). Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes.
  • Rubinstein, J. S., Meyer, D. E., & Evans, J. E. (2001). Executive control of cognitive processes in task switching. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance.
  • Mark, G., Gonzalez, V. M., & Harris, J. (2005). No task left behind? Examining the nature of fragmented work. Proceedings of CHI 2005.
  • Mark, G., Gudith, D., & Klocke, U. (2008). The cost of interrupted work: more speed and stress. Proceedings of CHI 2008.
