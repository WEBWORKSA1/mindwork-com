Sleep, exercise and thinking: the basics that beat every hack

What the research says about the two habits with the largest effect on how well you think, in practical terms. About 6 minutes to read.

No timer, app or technique on this site will do as much for your thinking as sleeping enough and moving regularly. The research on both is large and unusually consistent. This guide is general information for healthy adults, not medical advice; talk to a doctor about persistent sleep problems or before starting a new exercise programme if you have a health condition.

Sleep: what the loss costs

In a 2003 study led by Hans Van Dongen, volunteers were limited to six hours in bed a night for two weeks. Their attention lapses rose steadily, reaching the level of people who had been kept awake for a full night or more. The striking part was that they did not feel it: their own sleepiness ratings levelled off after a few days while their performance kept falling.

Being awake too long has a similar effect. Williamson and Feyer found that after 17 to 19 hours without sleep, performance on some tasks matched that of people with a blood alcohol level of 0.05%.

Sleep: what it does for memory

Sleep is when the brain stabilises what it learned during the day. Diekelmann and Born’s 2010 review describes how memories are replayed and strengthened during deep sleep. Practically: studying and then sleeping beats staying up to study more.

Sleep: what to do

  • Aim for at least seven hours. That is the adult recommendation from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society.
  • Keep a regular wake time, weekends included. It anchors everything else.
  • Get daylight early in the day and dim the lights in the last hour before bed.
  • Stop caffeine by early afternoon; it stays in your system for hours.

Exercise: the evidence

A single session helps a little, straight away. A 2012 meta-analysis by Chang and colleagues found a small, reliable improvement in thinking during and shortly after a bout of exercise.

Regular training does more. In a year-long trial by Kirk Erickson’s group, older adults who walked briskly three days a week increased the volume of the hippocampus, a region central to memory, by about 2%, while it shrank in a stretching-only control group. Memory performance improved along with it.

Exercise: what to do

  • Follow the World Health Organization guideline of at least 150 minutes a week of moderate aerobic activity, such as brisk walking.
  • Use a ten-minute walk as a break between focus blocks. It works better than a feed.
  • Put a walk before the hardest thinking of the day when you can.

Check it on yourself

Take the reaction time test at the same hour after a short night and after a full one. The difference is often 20 to 40 milliseconds, enough to see on the dashboard. Your own numbers are more persuasive than any study.
