The Pomodoro Technique: rules, variations and when not to use it

The original six steps, the two rules people skip, what the research says about timed breaks, and when a timer gets in the way. About 5 minutes to read.

Francesco Cirillo invented the Pomodoro Technique as a university student in the late 1980s. Struggling to concentrate, he challenged himself to study for ten minutes using a tomato-shaped kitchen timer (pomodoro is Italian for tomato). The method that grew from it is now the most widely used focus routine in the world.

The six steps

  1. Choose one task.
  2. Set a timer for 25 minutes.
  3. Work on the task until the timer rings.
  4. Mark one session done.
  5. Take a five-minute break.
  6. After four sessions, take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.

The focus timer on this site runs the whole cycle and counts sessions against your tasks.

Two rules people skip

A session is indivisible. If you abandon it, it does not count. This is what gives the timer its force: stopping at minute 18 to check a message throws away the 18 minutes.

Interruptions are handled, not obeyed. For an internal urge (“I should look that up”), write it on a list and carry on. For a person, Cirillo’s sequence is inform, negotiate, schedule, call back: tell them you are in the middle of something, agree a time, and return to them when the session ends.

Does it work?

The technique itself has been studied less than its popularity suggests, but its two ingredients have support. Ariga and Lleras found in 2011 that brief breaks during a long attention task prevented the usual decline in performance. In a 2023 study of students, Biwer and colleagues compared fixed, Pomodoro-style breaks with breaks taken whenever students felt like it. The fixed-break groups reported better concentration and motivation and less fatigue, and they finished similar work in less time.

Variations worth trying

  • 50 and 10 for programming, writing and analysis, where 25 minutes ends just as you get going.
  • 15 and 3 for tasks you dread. The aim is only to start.
  • Count, do not clock. Plan the day in sessions (“this report is four”) and compare your estimate with the result. Within two weeks your estimates get noticeably better.

When not to use it

  • When you are already absorbed and the work is flowing. A timer that breaks a good run is doing harm; let the session continue and take a longer break afterwards.
  • For meetings, calls and collaborative work, which run on other people’s clocks.
  • For open-ended creative thinking, where a walk often does more than a countdown.
