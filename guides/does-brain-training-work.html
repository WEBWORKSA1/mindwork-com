Does brain training work? What the evidence says

An honest reading of the research on brain games: what improves, what does not carry over, and what is worth your time. About 7 minutes to read.

This site offers cognitive tests, so you deserve a straight answer to the obvious question. The short version: practising a mental task makes you better at that task. Whether it makes you better at anything else is where the evidence gets thin.

Near transfer and far transfer

Researchers separate two kinds of benefit. Near transfer means improving on the trained task and tasks that closely resemble it. Far transfer means improving at different things: your job, your memory for names, your general reasoning. Near transfer is easy to produce. Far transfer is what the marketing promises.

The main studies

In 2010 Adrian Owen and colleagues ran a six-week online trial with 11,430 participants for the BBC. People improved on every task they trained, and showed no meaningful gain on untrained tests, even closely related ones.

In 2014 a group of about 70 scientists signed a statement, organised by the Stanford Center on Longevity, objecting to claims that brain games prevent cognitive decline. A second group of more than 100 scientists replied that the statement went too far. In 2016 Daniel Simons and six colleagues reviewed the studies cited by brain-training companies and concluded that there was extensive evidence of improvement on trained tasks, less for similar tasks, and little for everyday performance.

The same year, the makers of one of the best-known brain-training apps agreed to pay 2 million dollars to settle US Federal Trade Commission charges that their advertising about work, school and age-related decline was not backed by evidence.

The strongest case in favour

The ACTIVE trial followed about 2,800 older adults for ten years after short courses of memory, reasoning or speed-of-processing training. Trained groups kept an edge on the trained ability, and a later analysis linked speed training with a lower risk of dementia. It is one trial with a specific kind of training, and researchers still argue about how to read it, but it is the reason the question is not closed.

What this means for you

  • Use tests as a gauge. Your reaction time and Stroop score move with sleep, stress and fatigue. Tracking them tells you something about your state today. That is how we suggest you use the tests here.
  • Train skills you want for their own sake. Faster mental arithmetic is useful in itself. So is a memory technique. Neither needs to “raise your IQ” to be worth ten minutes.
  • Do not expect a game to replace the basics. The best-supported ways to protect thinking are unglamorous: aerobic exercise, sleep, and learning demanding new skills. In the Synapse Project, older adults who spent three months learning digital photography or quilting improved their memory, while a group doing easy, familiar activities did not.

Our position

The tests on this site are free measurements and short drills. They are not a treatment, and they will not make you smarter in general. Used for five minutes a day they are a decent warm-up and an honest mirror, and that is all we claim for them.
