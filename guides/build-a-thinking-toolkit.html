Build a thinking toolkit: how to use mental models at work

Why a handful of well-chosen models beats a long list, and a four-week plan for making five of them automatic. About 6 minutes to read.

A mental model is a compact idea about how something works, small enough to carry and general enough to reuse. “Work expands to fill the time available” is one. “A system moves as fast as its slowest step” is another. Each one lets you see a pattern faster than you could reason it out from scratch.

Why you need more than one

Abraham Maslow wrote in 1966 that if the only tool you have is a hammer, it is tempting to treat everything as a nail. A single model, however good, gets applied where it does not fit. Charlie Munger’s answer, in a 1994 talk to business students, was a “latticework” of models drawn from several disciplines, so that problems can be looked at from more than one side.

The opposite mistake is more common now: collecting a hundred models and using none. A model only helps if it comes to mind at the moment of decision.

Start with five

Pick one from each area where your work most often goes wrong. A reasonable starting set:

  • For choosing: reversible and irreversible decisions
  • For risk: the pre-mortem
  • For priorities: the Pareto principle
  • For process problems: bottlenecks
  • For communication: the pyramid principle

Attach each model to a trigger

Models fire when they are tied to situations. Write an if-then line for each: “If I am about to commit more than a week of someone’s time, then I ask whether it is a one-way door.” “If I am writing an update, then the first sentence is the conclusion.” Psychologists call these implementation intentions, and they are one of the more reliable ways to turn an intention into a habit.

A four-week plan

  1. Week 1. Read the five model pages. Write your five if-then lines on one card and keep it where you work.
  2. Week 2. Use each model once on a real decision, however small. Note in two lines what it changed.
  3. Week 3. Explain one model to a colleague using an example from your own team. If you cannot, reread it.
  4. Week 4. Review your notes. Keep the models that changed a decision, replace the ones that did not, and add one new model.

Remember what a model is

Alfred Korzybski’s phrase “the map is not the territory” applies to every model here, including that one. A model is a simplification that is useful for a purpose. When it stops fitting the facts in front of you, the facts win.
