How memory works, and how to make things stick

The three stages of memory in plain terms, and the four study techniques with the strongest evidence behind them. About 7 minutes to read.

Memory is not one thing and it is not a recording. It is a set of processes: taking information in, holding it briefly, storing some of it, and rebuilding it later. Each stage fails in its own way, and each has a known fix.

Three stages

Working memory is the small workspace where you hold what you are thinking about right now. George Miller’s famous estimate was about seven items; later work by Nelson Cowan puts it nearer four chunks. It lasts seconds unless you rehearse. The number memory test measures it directly.

Encoding moves information into long-term storage. What gets stored depends on how deeply you process it. Craik and Lockhart showed that thinking about what something means leaves a stronger trace than noticing how it looks or sounds.

Retrieval is the act of pulling a memory back, and it is reconstructive: you rebuild the memory each time from stored fragments. That is why confident memories can be wrong, and also why the act of recalling changes what is stored.

Why you forget

Hermann Ebbinghaus tested himself on lists of nonsense syllables in the 1880s and plotted how fast they faded. His forgetting curve drops steeply in the first day and then levels off. Most forgetting is not decay of a stored record; it is failure to encode deeply in the first place, or failure to find the route back.

Four techniques that work

In 2013 John Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed ten common study techniques. Two were rated high in usefulness. Two popular ones, highlighting and rereading, were rated low.

  1. Test yourself. Close the book and write down what you remember. Roediger and Karpicke found that students who practised recall remembered far more a week later than students who reread, even though the rereaders felt more confident.
  2. Space it out. Review after a day, then a few days, then a week. A meta-analysis by Cepeda and colleagues covering hundreds of experiments found spaced study consistently beat massed study. See spaced repetition.
  3. Explain it. Putting an idea into your own plain words forces deep processing and exposes gaps. That is the Feynman technique.
  4. Use places and pictures. The method of loci, placing vivid images along a familiar route, is the technique memory champions use. In a 2017 study by Martin Dresler’s group, ordinary volunteers who trained with it for six weeks more than doubled the number of words they could recall from a list.

A five-minute routine

After a meeting, a chapter or a course module: close the source, write everything you can recall for two minutes, check what you missed, and put three questions in your calendar for tomorrow, next week and next month. That is retrieval, feedback and spacing in one habit.

What does not help much

Rereading and highlighting feel productive because the material becomes familiar. Familiarity is not recall. If you can only recognise an idea when you see it, you have not learned it yet. Sleep, on the other hand, helps a great deal; see the basics guide.
