Better decisions under pressure: a working checklist

Six questions to ask before any decision that matters, with the research behind each one. About 6 minutes to read.

Most bad decisions at work are not caused by missing intelligence. They are caused by skipped steps: nobody asked what else could be done, what would prove the plan wrong, or what it would cost to reverse. A short checklist catches most of them. Atul Gawande’s work on surgical checklists made the general point: experts fail on the simple steps, under pressure, and a list on paper prevents it.

The six questions

  1. Can this be undone? Reversible decisions deserve speed; irreversible ones deserve care. Most are more reversible than they feel. See two-way doors.
  2. What are the other options? “Should we do X?” is half a question. Force a third option onto the table, including doing nothing. The decision matrix makes the comparison explicit.
  3. What usually happens? Before trusting your forecast for this project, ask how similar projects turned out. Daniel Kahneman calls this the outside view, and it is the best single correction for the planning fallacy.
  4. What would change my mind? Name the evidence that would make you drop the plan, then go and look for it. This is the working cure for confirmation bias.
  5. How does this fail? Run a pre-mortem: assume it failed and write why. Gary Klein introduced the method because people speak more freely about a failure that is treated as already certain.
  6. What happens after it works? Follow the consequences one step further. See second-order thinking.

Does it matter when you decide?

You may have read that judges grant parole less often as they get hungry. That finding, from a 2011 study by Danziger and colleagues, is real, but other researchers have argued that the order in which cases were scheduled explains much of the pattern. The broader theory behind it, that willpower is a fuel that runs down, also failed a large multi-laboratory replication in 2016.

So the strong claim is shaky. The modest version is safe: tired, rushed, hungry people rely more on defaults and first impressions. Put the decisions that matter in the part of the day when you are fresh, and do not make them at the end of a six-hour meeting.

Keep a decision journal

For each significant decision, write four lines: what you decided, what you expected to happen, why, and how confident you were. Review the entries every quarter. Without the record, hindsight rewrites your memory of what you believed, and you learn nothing. With it, you find out which kinds of decisions you are reliably good and bad at.

Groups

In meetings, have people write their view before anyone speaks, and let the most senior person speak last. Both steps cut anchoring and deference, and they cost two minutes.
